First rambling of the year just dropped and we are talking about baking. Out of many hobbies I've picked up over the past couple of years, baking has been something I've stuck with much longer than expected. As someone with no soft spots for sweets, I am quite surprised how well it has turned out so far and not to mention how fulfilling the whole hobby is. So grab your notes and strap in, we are about to discuss, reminisce and of course learn how not to bake.

As it turns out, to bake one needs an oven and we did not have one around. So I set out scrounging various sources, diving deep into forum threads and taking suggestions among friends. Here's what I had gathered till that point: What is an oven? It is like asking what is a fish. There's no set way of defining an oven, it could be anything from a hole in a wall to gas furnaces that smelt metal. I was asking the wrong question, the question I should be asking is, so what oven to get? See when I hear "oven", there are broadly three kinds I think of.

Conventional ovens! Those below the counter gas ovens you see in sitcoms, those are your conventional ovens. These bad boys are heavy duty workers that can hold temperatures for as long as you need and come pre fit in most western homes. When it comes to baking most recipes available online are modelled on western kitchens. Probably the most authentic way to bake would be using one of these conventional ovens.

Then comes its closest cousin, the convection oven. You have two heating elements, one up top and another at the bottom and a fan to get that hot air all over the compartment.

Lastly you have the most convenient and popular microwave. The heating element here is replaced by pulsating electromagnetic radiation and a rotating turntable inside the compartment to ensure equal distribution of heat. Although very accessible, not the most ideal tool to bake.

Now that leads us to my choice, too broke to get a gas oven installed but not shameless enough to resort to a microwave. A convection oven it is! After roaming a few stores, I realised I'd just Amazon one and save myself the hassle. I got myself what one would call an OTG, Oven + Toaster + Grill. It came with rotisserie functionality, an illuminated compartment and could house 30L. Pretty decent for the price I paid.

Now that the oven is sorted, where do we start? Like everyone else I started with cookies as well. Soon I realised baking is more of a math than _art_? So here's my experience and some tidbits I've gathered over the past couple of years.

## More butter makes everything better

If a recipe calls for 150 grams of butter show some love and put in 25 or 30 grams more. I find unsalted Amul butter bought off the shelf is just not _rich_ enough to elevate your batter so going with more butter than expected is a good option.

## Eggs are hard to substitute

I've tried my hand at vegan variants of standard recipes a few times, even if you do manage to substitute butter somehow, eggs are just unbeatable (pun intended). From what little expertise I have, eggs bind your ingredients so damn well that nothing comes close. Huge props to vegan chefs who manage to pull it off, I could never.

## A kitchen scale will change your life

As mentioned earlier, baking is math, math loves numbers, your scale shows you the said numbers. It is very easy not to flatten a measuring cup before adding ingredients. There's also a good chance that there are probably lumps in your flour taking up more volume than intended. Moreover room temperature butter and blocks of the same vary in volume not mass. Get a kitchen scale, probably 250 bucks on your favourite quick-com app.

## It's flour all the way down

Refined wheat flour or maida as you'd colloquially call it plays a very important role in how your dish turns out. For the longest time I stuck to standard loosely sold flour, this however needs to be sieved into your wet ingredients. Investing in a fine sieve is everything, you don't want lumps in your cake.

## Brown sugar is the only sugar you'll ever need to _buy_

Get yourself a kilo of brown sugar, if your recipe calls for caster sugar, baker's sugar and god knows how many variants of white sugar, just whip out your mixer and grind away however fine you want. The ever present crystal will get the job done and not to mention it's the most convenient to store as well.

## Grease your baking sheets

No matter what brand baking sheet you end up getting, they need to be greased, even if they claim otherwise just go ahead and lather it up.

## You can't go wrong with 175°C @ 30′

There's a reason most ovens come with a 175°C at 30 minute preset. If you are ever unsure how far to go, this is your number. Although there's a small caveat that you shouldn't be burning your cookies for that long.

## Freeze your dough overnight

I soon realised that most recipes call for a few hours of refrigeration but when I forgot the dough overnight one time, you would not believe how well they turned out. Now the game is to prepare dough at night and bake a fresh batch in the morning. Do remember that this advice doesn't apply for your leavened breads or anything with a leavening agent.

## Make notes and experiment

Everybody doesn't like the same variant of a brownie or a cookie or a cake, maybe you were a few ingredients short no matter the case, make notes, jot it down, write it down. The next time you set your pan you know just about what to change.
